Singapore’s central bank tightened its monetary policy settings, flagging that the surging energy prices caused by the Iran war could drive up inflation. One thing to note: Singapore’s primary monetary policy tool isn’t like most countries. Conventionally, most central banks control inflation through manipulating domestic interest rates. However, as Singapore is a highly open economy in which imports account for a significant proportion of domestic consumption, managing exchange rates is a more effective means of controlling imported inflation.

One of the main macroeconomic objectives is to keep inflation low–usually around 2%. Due to the war, oil prices have surged, resulting in cost-push inflation for Singaporean consumers. To combat this, Singapore increases its dollar’s rate of appreciation, strengthening it against other currencies worldwide. This means that oil can now be bought at a lower price. Now that prices are cheaper, it decreases Singapore’s inflation. 

The effects of this monetary policy can be seen through an AD/AS diagram. Thanks to the lower oil prices, it increases the productive capacity which domestic firms can produce, meaning the aggregate supply of Singapore would increase, shown through a rightward shift of the short-run aggregate supply (SRAS). Since SRAS shifts rightward, there is a new equilibrium where the price level is now lower for Singaporean consumers, resulting in lower inflation.

Even though these measures were put into place to try and negate the effects of the war, Singapore’s monetary authority stated “There are considerable risks around the outlook for inflation and growth”, and that “A more persistent disruption to energy supplies will exacerbate inflationary pressures worldwide, as well as deepen the drag on growth.”, saying that these measures put in place can negate only so much of the pressures caused by the war, further emphasizing the risk it has to Singapore’s economy, especially since some sectors are already experiencing economic contractions.
